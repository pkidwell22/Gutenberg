# The seventh day: providence and promised rest

**Editorial synthesis — selected historical readings, not a consensus verdict.**

Luther distinguishes completed creation from continuing preservation; Augustine reads the seventh day's rest as a promise of eternal life.

Passages: Genesis 2:1–3.

## Luther: rest is not abandonment

Luther handles the tension between Genesis's divine rest and Christ's statement that the Father still works. In the quoted paragraph, rest means no further design to create another heaven and earth, not cessation of preservation and government. His subsequent language separates creation-work from government-work.[2]

## Augustine: the Sabbath without evening

At the close of Confessions XIII, Augustine reads the seventh day, without a narrated evening, as announcing the believer's future rest in God. He also says that God ever works and ever rests: divine rest is not a new temporal state that follows exhaustion. The interpretation is eschatological and devotional, explicitly asking how the creation account speaks to human life.[4]

## Editorial comparison and limits

These excerpts differ in emphasis rather than forming a straightforward contradiction: Luther explains continued divine care for creation, and Augustine draws out promised participation in God's rest. Neither quoted position requires divine inactivity. The comparison does not establish that Luther excludes spiritual readings everywhere, or that Augustine abandons the created world for symbolism. Their wider accounts should remain open for further study.[2][4]

### Martin Luther — Commentary on Genesis, Vol. 1: Luther on the Creation

Chapter II, exposition of Genesis 2:2; paragraph beginning ‘My simple and plain reply’; frozen source lines 5154–5160.[2]

> My simple and plain reply to the above question is, that a solution of
> any difficulty that may be raised is furnished by the present text
> itself, when it says, "Thus the heavens and the earth were finished."
> The Sabbath or rest of the Sabbath here signifies that God so rested,
> as not to have any further design of creating any other heaven and
> earth. It does not signify that God ceased to preserve and govern the
> heaven and the earth, which he had now created and finished.

### Martin Luther — Commentary on Genesis, Vol. 1: Luther on the Creation

Chapter II, exposition of Genesis 2:2; paragraph beginning ‘The solution of the question’; frozen source lines 5177–5195.[2]

> The solution of the question now under consideration therefore is easy
> and plain. "God rested on the seventh day from the work which he had
> made;" that is, God was content with the earth and the heaven which he
> had created by the Word. He created not nor intended to create new
> heavens or new earths, nor new stars nor new trees. God nevertheless
> still works. He "worketh hitherto," as Christ says above. He forsakes
> not nature, which he once made "in the beginning;" but he preserves
> and governs it to this day, by the power of his Word. He has ceased
> from his creation-work, but he has not ceased from his
> government-work. The human race began in Adam. In the earth began by
> the Word the animal race, if I may so speak; in the sea, the race of
> fishes; and in the air, the race of birds. But the human race did not
> cease in Adam, nor did all other races cease in the first created
> animals of their kind. The Word originally spoken upon the human race
> still remains in all its power and efficiency. The word, "Be fruitful
> and multiply," ceases not nor ever will cease, nor the words, "Let the
> sea bring forth fishes," nor "Let the earth bring forth beasts and the
> air birds." The omnipotent power and efficacy of the original Word
> still preserves and governs the whole creation.

### Augustine of Hippo — Confessions

Confessions, Book XIII, concluding Sabbath meditation; paragraphs beginning ‘But the seventh day’ and ‘For then shalt Thou rest’; frozen source lines 9338–9351.[4]

> But the seventh day hath no evening, nor hath it setting; because Thou
> hast sanctified it to an everlasting continuance; that that which Thou
> didst after Thy works which were very good, resting the seventh day,
> although Thou madest them in unbroken rest, that may the voice of
> Thy Book announce beforehand unto us, that we also after our works
> (therefore very good, because Thou hast given them us), shall rest in
> Thee also in the Sabbath of eternal life.
> 
> For then shalt Thou rest in us, as now Thou workest in us; and so shall
> that be Thy rest through us, as these are Thy works through us. But
> Thou, Lord, ever workest, and art ever at rest. Nor dost Thou see in
> time, nor art moved in time, nor restest in a time; and yet Thou makest
> things seen in time, yea the times themselves, and the rest which
> results from time.
