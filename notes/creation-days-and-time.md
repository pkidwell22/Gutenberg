# Creation days and the beginning of time

**Editorial synthesis — selected historical readings, not a consensus verdict.**

Luther defends the narrated six days; Augustine asks whether a temporal ‘before creation’ is coherent. The questions overlap without being identical.

Passages: Genesis 1:1–5; Genesis 1:31–31; Genesis 2:1–3.

## Luther: the narrative's temporal sequence

In his general discussion of the six days, Luther explicitly rejects an allegorical or figurative replacement of the sequence: Moses means creation in six days. His admission that the reason for this sequence may remain incomprehensible is part of the argument, not permission to replace the text with a different chronology.[2]

## Augustine: time belongs to creation

In Confessions XI, Augustine challenges the imagined stretch of idle time before God made heaven and earth. If God creates time itself, asking what God did ‘then’ before time existed misstates the problem. This extract addresses the relation between the Creator and time; by itself it does not establish a particular length for Genesis's days.[4]

## Editorial comparison and limits

The comparison distinguishes narrative sequence from the metaphysics of time. Luther's insistence on six days should not be recast as a modern scientific argument; Augustine's refusal of a pre-creation ‘then’ should not be treated as denial of every temporal sequence within creation. Luther also describes God as independent of time in his discussion of Genesis 2:2, quoted here as a control against a false opposition. These are selected historical Christian readings, not a survey of contemporary cosmology or of all Augustine's Genesis works.[2][4]

### Martin Luther — Commentary on Genesis, Vol. 1: Luther on the Creation

Chapter I, I. Introduction; paragraph beginning ‘With respect therefore’; frozen source lines 1965–1970.[2]

> With respect therefore to this opinion of Augustine, we conclude that
> Moses spoke literally and plainly and neither allegorically nor
> figuratively; that is, he means that the world with all creatures was
> created in six days as he himself expresses it. If we cannot attain
> unto a comprehension of the reason why it was so, let us still remain
> scholars and leave all the preceptorship to the Holy Spirit!

### Augustine of Hippo — Confessions

Confessions, Book XI; paragraph beginning ‘But if any excursive brain’; frozen source lines 6944–6957.[4]

> But if any excursive brain rove over the images of forepassed times, and
> wonder that Thou the God Almighty and All-creating and All-supporting,
> Maker of heaven and earth, didst for innumerable ages forbear from so
> great a work, before Thou wouldest make it; let him awake and consider,
> that he wonders at false conceits. For whence could innumerable ages
> pass by, which Thou madest not, Thou the Author and Creator of all
> ages? or what times should there be, which were not made by Thee? or
> how should they pass by, if they never were? Seeing then Thou art the
> Creator of all times, if any time was before Thou madest heaven and
> earth, why say they that Thou didst forego working? For that very time
> didst Thou make, nor could times pass by, before Thou madest those
> times. But if before heaven and earth there was no time, why is it
> demanded, what Thou then didst? For there was no "then," when there was
> no time.

### Martin Luther — Commentary on Genesis, Vol. 1: Luther on the Creation

Chapter II, exposition of Genesis 2:2; paragraph beginning ‘With respect to Martin Luther’; frozen source lines 5210–5226.[2]

> With respect to Martin Luther before you. If you look at my individual
> person I am a certain kind of new creature; because sixty years ago I
> had no existence. This is the common thought and judgment of the
> world. But the thought and judgment of God are far different. For in
> God's sight I was begotten and commenced, being multiplied immediately
> "from the beginning of the world." When God said, "Let us make man,"
> he then created me also. For whatever God willed to create that he did
> create when he spoke the word. All things did not then appear indeed
> on a sudden before our existing eyes. For as the arrow or the ball
> from the cannon, in which is the greatest velocity attached to the
> works of men is in one moment directed to its mark, and yet does not
> reach that mark without a certain interval and space between, so God
> rushes, as it were by his Word, from "the beginning" to the end of the
> world. For with God there is no before nor afterwards; no swift nor
> slow; but all things to his eyes are at once present. For God is
> simply absolutely independent of and alone, and separate from all
> time!
